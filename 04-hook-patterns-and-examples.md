# Hook patterns and examples

Beyond the broad hook types, a handful of reusable opening patterns show up again and again because they work. These are fill-in-the-blank structures you can adapt to your topic: the mistake reveal, the number tease, the direct address, the in-progress open, and the stakes statement. Treat them as starting shapes, not scripts to copy word for word.

## The mistake reveal

This pattern opens by naming a common error the viewer might be making: "You are charging your phone wrong" or "Most people brush their teeth at the wrong time." It works because no one wants to be the person making the mistake, so they stay to check themselves. The video then explains the mistake and the fix. The key is that the mistake must be real and the correction genuinely useful, or the pattern feels like clickbait.

## The number tease

This pattern promises a specific, bounded payoff: "Three signs your resume gets ignored" or "The one setting that doubled my battery life." A concrete number sets expectations and creates a loop the viewer wants closed by reaching the items. Specificity, the one setting, the three signs, feels more credible and more finite than a vague promise. Deliver every item you promised, and consider saving the strongest for last so viewers stay to the end.

## Direct address and the in-progress open

Direct address points the hook straight at a defined viewer: "If you run more than twice a week, watch this." It self-selects exactly the people who should care and tells them the video is for them. The in-progress open drops the viewer into the middle of action already happening, no intro, the work already underway, so it looks like something is happening worth catching up on. Both skip the runway and put the viewer immediately inside the content.

## The stakes statement and how to combine

The stakes statement opens by naming what is at risk or what is to be gained: "This mistake could cost you your deposit" or "This takes five minutes and saves you hours." It makes the payoff feel worth the watch. In practice the strongest openings often combine patterns: a result-first visual under a number-tease line, or a contrarian claim aimed with direct address. Mix them, keep them honest, and always make sure the video pays off whatever the opening promised.
